# Soap-Making Basics + First Test Batch

A practical guide for making scent-free, natural cold-process soap at home. Written for a first-time maker.

## How soap actually works

Soap is what you get when fats/oils react with lye (sodium hydroxide, NaOH) in a process called **saponification**. Oil + lye → soap + glycerin. There is no such thing as real bar soap made without lye; when the reaction finishes correctly, **no lye remains** in the finished bar. Handmade cold-process soap keeps all its natural glycerin (commercial soap often strips it out), which is why it feels gentler.

Because the product is scent-free and natural, the recipe below uses nothing but oils, lye, and water. No fragrance, no essential oils, no colorants. That's a genuine selling point for sensitive skin, eczema, and fragrance-intolerant customers, and it also keeps the regulatory and allergy-label story simple.

## Why coconut oil, and why not only coconut oil

- **Coconut oil** makes a hard bar with big fluffy lather and strong cleansing power. But at high percentages it's *too* cleansing. It strips skin oils and feels drying.
- **Olive oil** makes a gentle, mild, conditioning bar with low, creamy lather. On its own (pure Castile) it takes 6-12 months to cure well and stays slimy-feeling early on.
- Blended, they cover each other's weaknesses. The classic beginner blend is roughly **70% olive / 30% coconut**, which is exactly what the test batch below uses.

Key term: **superfat**. This is extra oil beyond what the lye can react with, left in the bar to moisturize. 5% is standard. (A fun exception: 100% coconut soap made at 20% superfat, a possible second product, and it even lathers in hard water.)

## Safety (read this twice)

Lye is caustic. Respect it and this is a safe hobby; get sloppy and it burns.

1. **Always add lye to water, never water to lye.** Water-into-lye can flash-boil and erupt ("snow floats on the lake": the lye snow goes into the lake of water).
2. Mix lye solution in a ventilated area (open window, or outside). It heats to ~90°C on its own and gives off fumes for a minute or two. Don't breathe over the container.
3. Wear **goggles** (not just glasses) and **nitrile gloves**, long sleeves, closed shoes, from lye-mixing until cleanup is done.
4. **No aluminum, ever**: lye reacts violently with it. Use stainless steel, polypropylene plastic (recycle code 5), or heat-safe glass (glass can etch over time; plastic/stainless preferred).
5. No kids or pets in the room. Work when the kitchen is yours.
6. Skin contact: flush with plenty of cool running water for several minutes. Eyes: flush 15+ minutes and seek medical care.
7. Label your lye container and keep it sealed and high up. It's also sold as drain cleaner for a reason, and it absorbs moisture from air and clumps if left open.
8. Fresh soap batter and un-cured soap are still caustic. Wear gloves when unmolding and cutting for the first days. Wash tools with paper-towel wipe-down first, then hot soapy water (or just leave them a few days; the residue turns into soap and washes off easily).

## The test batch: "Bare Bar" 70/30 (unscented, ~7 bars)

**Total oils: 500 g. Superfat: 5%. Makes ~730 g of soap, about 6-7 bars.**

| Ingredient | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Olive oil | 350 g (70%) | Pure or pomace olive oil; doesn't need to be extra virgin |
| Coconut oil (76°F, refined) | 150 g (30%) | "76" refers to its melt point in °F; standard refined coconut oil from any grocery store works |
| Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), pure | 70.6 g | Must be 99%+ pure lye, no additives |
| Distilled water | 140-155 g | Distilled avoids mineral spots; ~2:1 water:lye |

**Before you start:** verify these numbers yourself at soapcalc.net or soapmakingfriend.com, punch in 350 g olive, 150 g coconut, 5% superfat. Recalculating every recipe through a lye calculator, every time, is the habit that keeps soap safe. Never trust a recipe (including this one) without checking it.

### Steps

1. **Suit up.** Goggles, gloves, sleeves. Clear the workspace, no distractions for the next hour.
2. **Weigh the water** into the PP plastic pitcher. **Weigh the lye** into a separate dry cup.
3. **Lye into water** (never the reverse), stirring gently with the stainless spoon until dissolved. It gets hot fast (~90°C). Set it somewhere safe to cool. Avoid the fumes for the first minute.
4. **Weigh the oils.** Melt the coconut oil gently (microwave in bursts or double boiler), then stir in the olive oil.
5. **Wait for both to cool to 38-43°C** (100-110°F), within ~10 degrees of each other. This takes 30-60 minutes, patience here, rushing causes most beginner failures.
6. **Pour the lye solution into the oils** slowly, down the shaft of the stick blender to avoid splashes.
7. **Blend in short bursts** (5-10 s on, stir, repeat). Within a few minutes the mixture turns from oily and separated to a smooth batter like thin custard. You've hit **"trace"** when drizzled batter leaves a faint trail on the surface before sinking back in.
8. **Pour into the mold**, tap it on the counter to release air bubbles, smooth the top.
9. **Insulate lightly**: cardboard over the top, a towel around it, and leave 24-48 hours. The soap will heat up on its own (gel phase); that's normal.
10. **Unmold and cut** (gloves on) into ~2.5 cm bars when firm, usually day 2-3 for this recipe.
11. **Cure 4-6 weeks** on a rack or cardboard with airflow, turning occasionally. Curing evaporates water and finishes the crystal structure: the bar gets harder, milder, and longer-lasting. This is the step that separates good soap from soft, short-lived soap. **Do not skip or shorten it for sales.**

### Is it safe? Testing the cured bar

- **Zap test** (the traditional maker's test): after 2+ weeks, wet a finger, rub the bar, touch finger to tongue tip. Fine soap tastes like soap; excess lye gives a distinct 9V-battery "zap." Zap = something went wrong, don't sell it.
- **pH strips**: cured soap normally reads pH 9-10.5. That's correct for real soap. It will never be pH 7. Anything over ~11 after a full cure is suspect.
- Keep a **batch log**: date, recipe, lye lot, temperatures, observations. Number every batch. This is both good practice and what a professional operation looks like if anyone ever asks.

### What can go wrong (and what it looks like)

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| White powdery film on top ("soda ash") | Air exposure while saponifying | Cosmetic only; steam or wash it off, or cover the mold with plastic wrap next time |
| Soft, won't harden | Too much water, or mismeasured lye | Wait longer; if still soft at 2 weeks, recheck your math and consider rebatch or discard |
| Oil pooling / separation | Under-blended, false trace | Usually fixable by rebatching (grate + gently reheat) |
| Crumbly, zaps | Too much lye | Discard. Scale or math error, recalibrate |
| Overheated crack down the middle | Too much insulation | Cosmetic; insulate less next time |

## Batch #2 candidate: 100% coconut "Salt & Sea"-style bar

Once 70/30 works: 500 g coconut oil, **20% superfat** (NaOH 73.2 g, water ~146 g). Hard white bar, huge lather. A natural fit for the coconut-focused brand and a good second SKU. (Optional: add 25-50% of oil weight in fine sea salt at trace for a salt bar, but cut it within 2-4 hours of pouring, salt bars harden like rock.)

## Scaling to production

- Scale by weight, recalculate lye every time. A 2.5 kg oil batch in two loaf molds ≈ 35 bars.
- Master batch your lye solution (pre-mix a large 1:1 lye:water stock) only once fully comfortable.
- Cost per bar at small scale lands around **$1.20-2.00** in materials for this recipe; scent-free means no essential-oil cost, which is normally the most expensive ingredient. Retail handmade bars in BC run **$7-10**. The margins are genuinely good.
